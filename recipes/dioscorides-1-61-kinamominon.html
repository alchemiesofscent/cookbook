Cinnamon unguent

draft

Cinnamon unguent is made from ben oil, with balsam wood, sweet flag and camel hay as the astringent ingredients and cinnamon, balsam fruit and myrrh as the aromatics: four times as much myrrh as cinnamon. Honey is mixed in during the kneading. No other amounts are given.

What to aim for

  • A good one is not acrid but mild in scent, with a hint of myrrh; thick, very fragrant, and bitter to the taste.
  • Its thickness should come from myrrh, not resin: resin gives neither bitterness nor fragrance.

Uses

Intensely acrid, warming and bitter: it opens passages, disperses, diffuses, and draws fluids and pneuma to itself, but makes the head heavy; with twice as much oil, wax and marrow, for disorders of the uterus; with cardamom, for fistulas, putrefactions, hydroceles, carbuncles and gangrenes; as an ointment, for recurrent chills, tremors and venomous bites; with ground unripe figs, a poultice for scorpion stings and spider bites.

English · approved translation

Cinnamon unguent is prepared from ben oil; astringent ingredients consisting of balsam wood, sweet flag, and camel hay; and aromatic ingredients consisting of cinnamon, balsam fruit, and four times as much myrrh as cinnamon. Honey is also mixed in during the kneading. A satisfactory specimen is not acrid but mild in scent, with a suggestion of myrrh; it is thick, intensely fragrant, and bitter to the taste. Such an unguent derives its thickness not from resin but from myrrh, since resin contributes neither bitterness nor fragrance.

Its capacity is intensely acrid, warming, and bitter. Accordingly, as it warms, it opens passages, disperses, diffuses, and draws fluids and pneuma toward itself; but it causes heaviness in the head. Mixed with twice as much oil, together with wax and marrow, it is also effective for disorders around the uterus. In this form it loses much of its acridity and becomes emollient; otherwise it burns and hardens more violently than any other thick unguent. With cardamom it is manifestly effective for fistulas and putrefactions, hydroceles, carbuncles, and gangrenes. As an ointment it is also effective against recurrent chills and tremors and for people bitten by venomous animals; mixed with finely ground unripe figs, it is applied as a poultice to those stung by scorpions or bitten by venomous spiders.

Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.61, ed. Wellmann.

For review

  • Myrrh (σμύρνης): Flag four times as much as the cinnamon, which has no amount: no unit. The Greek prints παράὰ (sic), presumably for παρὰ

Editor's notes

The whole chapter is one recipe; nothing is cut. Wellmann prints the section number 2 inside a sentence (καὶ 2 πικράν); the English starts its second paragraph at 'Its capacity'.

The text names no amounts except the myrrh, given relative to the cinnamon.

ῥητίνη is named only to say the unguent's thickness comes from myrrh, not resin; it is not an ingredient and is not listed.
