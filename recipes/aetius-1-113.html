Rose oil

draft · Rose oil

ml of unripe-olive oil the written batch

The simple rose oil: red roses, their calyces removed and cooled for a day and a night, are put into unripe-olive oil in a jar tied shut with linen and hide, and set in the sun for twenty days. The oil is then strained and the jars stored on boards in rooms of even temperature.

TimeCool the roses a day and a nightSun twenty daysNo temperatures are given beyond rooms of even temperature for storing. In the well method the jar stays down forty days.

Method

  1. 1

    Prepare the roses. Take three unciae of red roses. Remove the calyces and leave them to cool for a day and a night.

    Calyces removed

    The Greek ἐξωνυχισμένων is the word Paul uses (ὠνυχισμένων), which his translation renders "nail-like bases removed".

  2. 2

    Add the oil and seal. Put the roses into a jar with one Italian sextarius of omphakine oil. Tie the mouth of the jar shut, inside with linen and outside with hide, in case of sudden showers.

  3. 3

    Sun. Set the jar in the sun for twenty days.

    Twenty days

    Paul (7.20.4) gives forty days in the sun for the same oil.

  4. 4

    Strain and store. Strain it, then set the jars on boards in rooms of even temperature.

Other ways

In a well. Some do not sun it, but lower the jar into a well of cold water for forty days.

With other ingredients. Some add other ingredients to the roses, but roses and oil alone make the best preparation.

What to aim for

  • Roses and oil alone make the best preparation.

Uses

For a head made hot and dry by sun, fever or the like: it moistens, soothes and brings on sleep; the finest, drunk with cold or warm water, for a stomach bitten by sharp bile or worms; with a wine decoction, against worms, especially in children; injected into the colon; not to be used where there is little heat and the body has excess.

Greek · Olivieri

Ῥόδινον σκευάζεται οὕτως· ῥόδων ἐρυθρῶν ἐξωνυχισμένων καὶ ἐψυγμένων ἡμέραν καὶ νύκτα 𐆄 γ, ἐλαίου ὀμφακίνου ξέστης ἰταλικὸς εἷς, ἐμβάλλοντα δὲ τὰ ῥόδα περισφίγγειν χρὴ τὸ στόμα τοῦ βίκου ἔσωθεν μὲν ὀθονίῳ, ἔξωθεν δὲ δέρματι διὰ τοὺς γιγνομένους ὄμβρους αἰφνίδιον καὶ ἡλιοῦν ἡμέρας κ καὶ οὕτως σειρώσαντα ἀποτίθεσθαι τοὺς βίκους ἐπὶ σανίδων ἐν οἴκοις εὐκράτοις. τινὲς δὲ ἕτερά τινα προσεμβάλλουσι τοῖς ῥόδοις. ἀρίστη δέ ἐστιν ἡ διὰ τῶν ῥόδων μόνων καὶ ἐλαίου σκευασία. τινὲς δὲ οὐχ ἡλιοῦσιν, ἀλλ' ἀποκρημνοῦσι τὸν βίκον εἰς φρέαρ ὕδατος ψυχροῦ ἡμέρας μ. ἁρμόζει δὲ κεφαλῇ θερμανθείσῃ καὶ ξηρανθείσῃ ἢ ἐξ ἡλιώσεως ἢ ἐκ πυρετῶν ἤ τινος ἄλλης τοιαύτης προφάσεως. ὑγραίνει γὰρ καὶ παρηγορεῖ καὶ ὕπνον ἐπάγει. […] καὶ πινόμενον δὲ τὸ κάλλιστον ῥόδινον σὺν ὕδατι ψυχρῷ ἢ θερμῷ ἐπὶ τῶν δακνομένων τὸν στόμαχον ἐκ χολῆς δριμείας ἢ ἐλμίνθων ἢ ἀσκαρίδων μεγάλας παρέχει ὠφελείας. ἐπὶ δὲ ἐλμίνθων καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ παίδων κρεῖττον ποιεῖ μιγνύμενον ἑψήματι τῷ ἀπ' οἴνου τριτουμένου σκευαζομένῳ καὶ πινόμενον· ἐκτινάσσει γὰρ αὐτὰς τάχιστα. ἐνίεται δὲ καὶ κώλῳ δακνομένῳ διὰ τὰς εἰρημένας προφάσεις. παραφυλακτέον δὲ τὴν χρῆσιν αὐτοῦ ἐφ' ὧν μὴ πολλή ἐστιν ἡ θερμασία καὶ περιττὸν τὸ σῶμα.

English · approved translation

Rose oil is prepared as follows: take three unciae of red roses with the calyces removed and cooled for a day and a night, and one Italian sextarius of omphakine oil. When the roses are put in, the mouth of the jar must be tied shut, inside with linen and outside with hide because sudden showers may occur, and it should be exposed to the sun for twenty days. After straining it, set the jars on boards in rooms of even temperature. Some people add other ingredients to the roses, but preparation from roses alone and oil is best. Some do not expose it to the sun, but lower the jar into a well of cold water for forty days. It is suitable for a head that has become hot and dry from sun exposure, fevers, or some other similar cause, for it moistens, soothes, and brings on sleep. […] The finest rose oil, when drunk with cold or warm water, gives great benefit to people whose stomach is bitten by sharp bile, intestinal worms, or large roundworms. For worms, especially in children, it works better when mixed with a decoction prepared from wine diluted to one-third strength and drunk, for it expels them very quickly. It is also injected into the colon when it is being bitten for the causes already mentioned. Its use must be avoided in patients where there is not much heat and the body has excess.

Source

Aetius of Amida, Libri medicinales 1.113, ed. Olivieri.

Editor's notes

Olivieri's Greek is printed in lines with line-end hyphens; the lines are joined here by script (line-end hyphens removed, other line breaks turned into spaces).

The chapter heading Ἔλαιον ῥόδινον, κηρωτὴ ἡ ψύχουσα ('Rose oil; the cooling wax-salve.') is left out, since it names the wax-salve too; the text starts at Ῥόδινον σκευάζεται οὕτως.

[…] marks an omission in both Greek and English: the cooling wax-salve (κηρωτή) made with the oil, from καὶ ἐπὶ σπλάγχνων to τοῦ ε λόγου (perfume-tables keeps it as aetius-1-113-2), and Olivieri's bracketed doublet [πλυνομένη … σπλάγχνοις]. The closing passage on drinking the finest rose oil (τὸ κάλλιστον ῥόδινον) is kept as part of the oil's uses.

Some add other ingredients to the roses; the text says roses and oil alone make the best preparation.

The Italian sextarius (ξέστης ἰταλικός) is taken to be the xestes of units.json.

Twenty days in the sun (κ) here against forty in Paul 7.20.4 (μ); the well alternative is forty days in both.

Plain step 6 (the well instead of the sun) is moved to other_ways; plain steps 4 and 5 (strain, store) are merged.
