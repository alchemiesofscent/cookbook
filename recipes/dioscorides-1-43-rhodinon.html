Rose oil

draft · Rose oil

g of olive oil the written batch

Olive oil is first boiled with schoinos and strained. The petals of a thousand roses are steeped in it overnight, worked with honey-coated hands, and pressed out. The pressed petals are pressed again with more of the prepared oil, giving lesser grades; for a stronger perfume, fresh roses are steeped in the first oil again, up to seven times.

Method

  1. 1

    Prepare the oil. Pound the schoinos and mix it with water. Boil it with the oil, stirring. Strain the schoinos off.

  2. 2

    Infuse the roses. Add the petals of a thousand fresh roses, picked dry, to the strained oil. Coat your hands with fragrant honey, then stir many times, pressing the petals down gently.

    Picked dry

    Fresh roses with no water on them: no dew, rain or washing. Water left in the oil spoils it (step 7).

  3. 3

    Press, settle, store. Leave it overnight, then press it out. When the sediment has settled, move the oil to a clean vessel. Store it in a mixing-bowl coated inside with honey. This is the first grade.

    The press

    The press and its trough are named only in step 7, but every pressing needs them.

  4. 4

    Press the petals again. Put the pressed petals in a small tub, pour 8 lb 3 oz of the prepared oil over them, and press again. This is the second grade.

    How much oil to prepare

    The text never says. All of the first 20 lb 5 oz goes into the first infusion, but this pressing needs 8 lb 3 oz more (one manuscript says 20). Prepare extra oil if you want lower grades. At the same proportion, that is about 2 lb 3 oz (0.7 kg) of schoinos to 8 lb 3 oz of oil.

  5. 5

    Further grades. If you wish, soak and press a third and a fourth time; the perfume comes in four grades. Coat each storage bowl with honey first.

  6. 6

    A stronger oil. Add the same number of fresh roses, picked dry, to the first-pressed oil. Stir with honey-coated hands and press out. Make the second, third and fourth grades the same way, with fresh roses each time; the oil grows stronger.

    Two kinds of repeat

    Pressing the same petals again (steps 4–5) gives lower grades; adding fresh roses to the first oil (step 6) makes it stronger.

  7. 7

    Every time. The oil takes up to seven infusions of roses, and no more. Coat the press trough with honey too. Separate the oil carefully from the rose juice: even a trace left in spoils the perfume.

Other ways

Steeped, not pressed. Some bruise the roses on their own and steep them in the oil, changing them over seven days, up to a third soaking, and store it like that.

Over seven days: how often the roses are changed is not stated; daily is possible.

A different preparation. Some prepare the oil with kalamos and aspalathos as well. Some also add anchousa, a dye-root, for colour, and salt so that it does not spoil.

Kalamos is read as κάλαμος ἀρωματικός, the aromatic reed; its identification is disputed.

A measured version. Manuscripts H, Da. Added after the thousand roses: “or 1 lb of kypeiros, 1 lb of oil, 1 lb of kalamos, 6 xestai of oil, 1½ xestai of roses.” Roses and oil are measured by volume.

ἢ κυπείρου λίτραν α΄, ἐλαίου λίτραν α΄, καλάμου λίτραν α΄, ἐλαίου ξέστας ϛ΄, ῥόδων ξέστην αϛ΄

In the apparatus of Wellmann's edition, not his text; Sprengel prints it in brackets, reading ἑλενίου (elecampane) for the first ἐλαίου.

What to aim for

  • It comes in four grades, from the first pressing to the fourth.
  • Fresh roses steeped in the first oil make it stronger, up to seven infusions; after that the oil takes no more.
  • Even the least of the rose juice left in the oil spoils the perfume.
  • Some add anchousa for a good colour, and salt so that it does not spoil.

The little root of anchousa also contributes to the colour of rose oil, and of iris oil.

Συνεργεῖ δὲ καὶ τῆς ἀγχούσης τὸ ῥιζίον εἰς τὴν χρόαν τοῦ ῥοδίνου καὶ τῆς ἴριδος Theophrastus, De odoribus 33

Colour is a third element, neglected by many; cinnabar and anchusa are added for its sake. Salt sprinkled in checks the nature of the oil.

tertius inter haec est colos multis neglectus; huius causa adduntur cinnabaris et anchusa. sal adspersus olei naturam coercet. Pliny, Natural history 13.7

Rose perfume is made white (candidum); kyprinon green, susinum oily, the Mendesian black, myrrh perfume pale.

cyprino viride fit, susino unguinosum, Mendesio nigrum, rhodino candidum, murra pallidum. Pliny, Natural history 13.17

Uses

Astringent and cooling: for wet dressings and poultices; drunk, it loosens the bowels and quenches burning in the stomach; for hollow and malignant ulcers, scurf and eruptions, the onset of headache, toothache, hardened eyelids, and by irrigation for the intestines and the uterus.

English · approved translation

1. Take five litrai and eight ounces of rush and twenty litrai and five ounces of oil. Chop the rush, knead it with water, and boil it while stirring; then strain. Put into the twenty litrai and five ounces of oil the petals from one thousand roses, without first wetting them. Coat your hands with fragrant honey and stir repeatedly, pressing gently. Leave it overnight and then press it out. Once the sediment has settled, transfer the oil to another receiving vessel and store it in a mixing bowl coated with honey. Place the pressed rose petals in a small washing basin, pour over them eight litrai and three ounces of the oil that has been made astringent, and press them out again.

2. This will give you the second oil. If you wish, continue pouring oil over the petals and pressing them out through a third and a fourth steeping; in this way first, second, third, and fourth grades of the perfume are produced. Whenever you do this, first coat the mixing bowls with honey. If you wish to make a second addition of petals, put the same number of fresh, unwetted rose petals into the oil from the first pressing. Stir them with hands previously coated with honey, press them out, and produce the second, third, and fourth oils by pressing in the same way. Each time you do this, add fresh roses, since the oil becomes more potent.

3. The oil can receive additions of rose petals through a seventh steeping, but no further. The pressing vat too should be coated with honey. The oil must be separated carefully from the rose juice, since even the smallest amount left mixed with it spoils the perfume. Some people crush the roses alone, steep them in oil, and replace them every seven days; after the third steeping they store the oil in this form. Others first make the oil astringent by adding calamus and aspalathos; some also add alkanet to give it an attractive color and salt to prevent spoilage.

4. It has an astringent and cooling capacity and is suitable for wet dressings and mixtures used in poultices. When given as a drink, it loosens the bowels and quenches burning in the stomach. It also promotes the filling in of hollow ulcers, soothes malignant ulcers, serves as an ointment for scurf and eruptions, as a wet dressing at the onset of headache, and as a rinse for toothache. Applied as an ointment, it is also effective for hardened eyelids; administered by irrigation, it is useful for irritations of the intestines and inflammations of the uterus.

Source

Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.43, ed. Wellmann.
