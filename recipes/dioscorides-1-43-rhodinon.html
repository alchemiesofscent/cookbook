ῥόδινον rhodinon

Rose oil

Rose oil family8 ingredientsTime · boil, rest

g of ἐλαίου (oil) as written

Olive oil is first boiled with schoinos and strained. The petals of a thousand roses are steeped in it overnight, worked with honey-coated hands, and pressed out. The pressed petals are pressed again with more of the astringed oil, giving lesser grades; for a stronger perfume, fresh roses are steeped in the first oil again, up to seven times.

Time

Boil
once, with the schoinos; no duration given
Rest
overnight, for each infusion

Apart from one boil and an overnight rest, no times or temperatures are given.

Method

  1. Prepare the oil Pound the schoinos and mix it with water. Boil it with the oil, stirring. Strain the schoinos off.

  2. Infuse the roses Add the petals of a thousand fresh roses, picked dry, to the strained oil. Coat your hands with fragrant honey, then stir many times, pressing the petals down gently.

    Picked dry Fresh roses with no water on them: no dew, rain or washing. Water left in the oil spoils it (step 7).

  3. Press, settle, store Leave it overnight, then press it out. When the sediment has settled, move the oil to a clean vessel. Store it in a mixing-bowl coated inside with honey. This is the first grade.

    The press The press and its trough are named only in step 7, but every pressing needs them.

  4. Press the petals again Put the pressed petals in a small tub, pour 8 lb 3 oz (2.7 kg) of the astringed oil over them, and press again. This is the second grade.

    How much oil to prepare All of the first 20 lb 5 oz goes into the first infusion, but this pressing needs 8 lb 3 oz more (one manuscript says 20). Prepare extra oil if you want lower grades. At the same proportion, that is about 2 lb 3 oz (0.7 kg) of schoinos to 8 lb 3 oz of oil.

  5. Further grades Soak and press a third and a fourth time, if you wish; the perfume comes in four grades. Coat each storage bowl with honey first.

  6. A stronger oil Add the same number of fresh roses, picked dry, to the first-pressed oil. Stir with honey-coated hands and press out. Make the second, third and fourth grades the same way, with fresh roses each time; the oil grows stronger.

    Two kinds of repeat Pressing the same petals again (steps 4–5) gives lower grades; adding fresh roses to the first oil (step 6) makes it stronger.

  7. Every time Give the oil no more than seven infusions of roses. Coat the press trough with honey too. Separate the oil carefully from the rose juice: even a trace left in spoils the perfume.

Other ways

Steeped, not pressed Some bruise the roses on their own and steep them in the oil, changing them over seven days, up to a third soaking, and store it like that.

Over seven days: how often the roses are changed is not stated; daily is possible.

A different preparation Some prepare the oil with kalamos and aspalathos as well. Some also add anchousa, a dye-root, for colour, and salt so that it does not spoil.

Kalamos is read as κάλαμος ἀρωματικός, the aromatic reed; its identification is disputed.

A measured version Added after the thousand roses: “or 1 lb of kypeiros, 1 lb of oil, 1 lb of kalamos, 6 xestai of oil, 1½ xestai of roses.” Roses and oil are measured by volume.

In the apparatus of Wellmann's edition, not his text; Sprengel prints it in brackets, reading ἑλενίου (elecampane) for the first ἐλαίου.

What to aim for

  • Dioscorides counts four grades, from the first pressing to the fourth.
  • According to Dioscorides, fresh roses steeped in the first oil make it stronger, up to seven infusions; after that the oil takes no more.
  • Dioscorides warns that even the least of the rose juice left in the oil spoils the perfume.
  • Dioscorides notes that some add anchousa for a good colour, and salt so that it does not spoil.

The little root of anchousa also contributes to the colour of rose oil, and of iris oil.

Συνεργεῖ δὲ καὶ τῆς ἀγχούσης τὸ ῥιζίον εἰς τὴν χρόαν τοῦ ῥοδίνου καὶ τῆς ἴριδος Theophrastus, De odoribus 33

Colour is a third element, neglected by many; cinnabar and anchusa are added for its sake. Salt sprinkled in checks the nature of the oil.

tertius inter haec est colos multis neglectus; huius causa adduntur cinnabaris et anchusa. sal adspersus olei naturam coercet. Pliny, Natural history 13.7

Rose perfume is made white (candidum); kyprinon green, susinum oily, the Mendesian black, myrrh perfume pale.

cyprino viride fit, susino unguinosum, Mendesio nigrum, rhodino candidum, murra pallidum. Pliny, Natural history 13.17
